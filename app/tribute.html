A tribute

The Anthem Makers

In Spore's Civilization stage, every nation could write its own anthem: pick a beat, draw a melody, add up to four ambient layers, and hear it play across your cities. It took a few seconds to use. It took a composer, an audio director, a small team of engineers and an open-source music language to make.

The people

Press ♪ on a card to hear a sound from the game connected to that person's work, played straight from your own copy of Spore.

Brian Eno

Music design · sound sources

The musician who named "ambient music" designed Spore's generative score. He spent time with the audio team teaching their note generator to fall into proper scales and modes. The ambient textures behind the anthem editor are his.

Wikipedia

Kent Jolly

Audio Director

Jolly led Spore's sound design from about five years before release. He studied electronic music at Mills College and had worked on The Sims and SimCity 3000. With Eno and Aaron McLeran he built the procedural music system, and he helped conceive the anthem's note editor.

His GDC 2008 talk

Aaron McLeran

Procedural music · civilization beats

McLeran worked on the procedural music with Jolly and Eno, including "the Shuffler", which assembles the soundtrack from fragments. With Jolly he made the Civilization-stage drum beats, which were then reprocessed into the loops you pick in the Beat panel.

GDC 2008 talk

Cyril Saint Girons

Audio engineer · anthem editor

He joined Spore's audio team in January 2007, after building EA's procedural audio tool in its R&D group. He conceived the note editor in stages with Jolly, Eno and Wright. His hardest problem was giving musicians enough control while keeping it intuitive for everyone else. He cited Electroplankton as an inspiration, and he presented the official anthem tutorial.

His tutorial video

Justin Graham

Lead audio engineer

He led the engineering side of an audio department that, by Saint Girons's account, included an audio director, a producer, two sound designers, two engineers and freelance contributors. That team shipped a game whose every sound runs through a live music program.

Will Wright

Creator of Spore · Maxis

Spore's designer shared Eno's fascination with simple rules producing endless variety. The two were first hooked on it by Conway's Game of Life. He was part of the conversations that shaped the note editor.

Playing with Time

Miller Puckette

Creator of Pure Data

All of Spore's audio runs on a modified version of Pure Data, the free, open-source visual music language Puckette created. So in Spore you're never listening to a recording, you're listening to a program. This studio runs those same Pd patches.

How it worked, and how this studio works

A program, not a recording

The anthem is a Pure Data patch inside Spore's audio files. It restarts drum loops on the downbeat, shuffles through slices, starts your melody on the beat, and drifts ambient layers in and out. The game tells it what you picked, and the patch does the rest.

Your notes, the game's rules

The editor's rules live in a small tuning file: two octaves of C major, up to 16 notes, lengths from a sixteenth to a whole note, and a table of which scale degree likes to follow which. "Generate new anthem" walks that table.

Rebuilt with respect

This studio runs the original patches unchanged, with the original sounds, layouts and art, all read from your own copy of Spore. It was made for personal use, as a thank-you.
